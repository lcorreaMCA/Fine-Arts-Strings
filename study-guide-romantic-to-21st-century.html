How to use this guide

The test has 30 questions worth 35 points: 25 multiple choice (1 point each) and 5 short answers (2 points each). Most questions are about music, and a few are about literature, painting, theater, and dance.

  • Read one lesson section a day, and cover the right column of each table to quiz yourself.
  • Learn the bold names and terms: each one can show up on the test.
  • Practice the five short-answer prompts at the end. Write 2 to 4 complete sentences with at least one specific example.
  • Use your Words to Know lists in each lesson for extra review.

The Romantic Period, Part 1: literature, art, theater, and dance (about 1820–1900)

Romanticism valued emotion, imagination, and individuality more than order, balance, and logic. Classical artists spoke to the head; Romantic artists spoke to the heart.

Who or whatRemember this
Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818)Began writing it at eighteen; often called the first science-fiction novel
The Brontë sisters and George SandWomen writers who published under male pen names
Caspar David FriedrichTiny, lonely figures before huge skies; the feeling of awe is the sublime
John Constable and J. M. W. TurnerConstable painted the English countryside; Turner painted swirling light and color
RealismPainting life as it really is, like Millet’s The Gleaners
Rosa BonheurFamous painter of animals; needed a police permit to wear trousers
ImpressionismNamed after Claude Monet’s painting Impression, Sunrise; quick, colorful impressions of light
Berthe Morisot and Mary CassattWomen Impressionists; Cassatt painted women and children
MelodramaPopular plays with music throughout, a hero, a villain, and lots of spectacle
Henrik Ibsen, A Doll’s HouseRealistic play; Nora leaves to find out who she really is
En pointeDancing on the very tips of the toes
Marie Taglioni, La Sylphide (1832)First great Romantic ballerina; her bell-shaped white skirt became the Romantic tutu

The Romantic Period, Part 2: music and composers

Romantic composers kept Classical forms like the symphony but made them longer, louder, and larger to express their feelings.

Classical (about 1750–1820)Romantic (about 1820–1900)
Order, balance, clarity: the headEmotion and self-expression: the heart
Short, balanced phrasesLong, sweeping melodies
Dynamics from pp to ffHuge contrasts, from pppp to ffff
Steady tempoFlexible tempo with rubato
Symphony of about 25 minutesSymphony of about 45 minutes or more
Orchestra of about 20 to 60 playersClose to 100 players by the end of the century
Who or whatRemember this
Ludwig van BeethovenThe bridge from Classical to Romantic; kept composing after he became completely deaf
Fate motifShort-short-short-LONG, the idea that opens Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony
RubatoSlightly holding back or pushing forward the tempo to be more expressive
Franz SchubertMaster of the art song (solo voice and piano); wrote more than 600 songs, including The Erlking
Frédéric ChopinWrote almost only for the piano; Polish mazurkas and polonaises; dreamy nocturnes
Franz LisztSuperstar pianist; his fans’ excitement was called Lisztomania
Louis Moreau GottschalkPianist born in New Orleans; Bamboula was inspired by dances in Congo Square
Clara SchumannOne of the greatest pianists of the 1800s; toured for 61 years and composed
Fanny Mendelssohn HenselWrote more than 450 pieces; some were published under her brother Felix’s name
Program musicInstrumental music that tells a story, like Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique and its idée fixe
NationalismLove of one’s homeland, as in Smetana’s The Moldau (a Czech river) and Dvořák’s New World Symphony
Opera words: libretto, aria, sopranoThe words of an opera; a solo song about feelings; the highest female voice
LeitmotifA short musical idea for a character or object, used by Richard Wagner
Giuseppe VerdiMost popular opera composer; his chorus “Va, pensiero” became a song of Italian independence

Modernism: Riot at the Théâtre (about 1890–1945)

Modernism was a break with tradition by artists who felt the old, pretty ways could not express a fast-changing world. There was no single style, and the boldest artists were called the avant-garde.

Who or whatRemember this
ExpressionismShows intense inner feelings, like Munch’s The Scream
CubismPicasso and Braque broke objects into flat, angular pieces seen from several angles at once
Abstract artShows moods, shapes, and colors instead of objects, like Kandinsky’s paintings
Claude DebussyBrought Impressionism into music; misty sounds, new scales, love of tone color
Prelude to the Afternoon of a FaunDebussy’s piece that opens with a dreamy solo flute
Whole-tone and pentatonic scalesSix notes all a whole step apart; five notes, like the black keys of a piano
Igor Stravinsky, The Rite of SpringBallet that caused a riot in Paris in 1913: pounding rhythms, clashing chords, stomping dancers
Serge Diaghilev and Vaslav NijinskyThe impresario of the Ballets Russes; the dancer who choreographed The Rite
Arnold SchoenbergWrote atonal music (no home key) and invented the twelve-tone system
SprechstimmeHalfway between speaking and singing, used in Pierrot lunaire
Dada and SurrealismDada celebrated nonsense and chance; Surrealism mixed reality with dreams
Neoclassicism“Back to Bach”: old forms with modern sounds, like Stravinsky’s Pulcinella
Béla BartókRecorded folk songs in Hungarian villages; used the piano like a drum
Louis Armstrong, George GershwinNew Orleans jazz star; Rhapsody in Blue opens with a clarinet glissando
William Grant StillHis Afro-American Symphony was the first work by a Black composer played by a major American orchestra
Florence PriceFirst Black woman to have a symphony played by a major American orchestra (Chicago, 1933)
Aaron Copland, Appalachian SpringBallet for Martha Graham that uses the Shaker hymn Simple Gifts

Jazz and Broadway

Jazz was born in New Orleans in the early 1900s, where many cultures shared their music. The Broadway musical grew from variety shows into stories told through song and dance.

The ingredients of jazz

IngredientRemember this
Congo SquareWhere enslaved Africans and free people of color gathered on Sundays to drum and dance
Brass bands and the second lineParade bands; the second line is the crowd dancing behind them
RagtimeSyncopated piano music; Scott Joplin was the “King of Ragtime”
BluesA 12-bar pattern with bent “blue notes”; Bessie Smith was the “Empress of the Blues”
Call and responseOne voice answered by others, from West African music and church services

Jazz and Broadway people and terms

Who or whatRemember this
Syncopation and improvisationAccents between the main beats; making up music on the spot
Louis ArmstrongBorn in New Orleans in 1901; jazz’s first superstar; made scat singing famous
SwingBig-band jazz people danced to in the 1930s and 1940s, led by Duke Ellington and Benny Goodman
BebopFast, complex jazz for listening, led by Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie
Book musicalA musical where the songs help tell the story; vaudeville was a variety show with no story
Show Boat (1927)Told a serious story about racism, with songs that fit the characters
Oklahoma! (1943)Rodgers and Hammerstein; started Broadway’s Golden Age
“I Want” songEarly song where the main character says what they want most
West Side Story (1957)Retells Shakespeare’s Romeo and Juliet with two New York gangs
Disney on BroadwayBeauty and the Beast (1994) was first; Julie Taymor directed The Lion King (1997)

Postmodernism: The Jungle of Styles (second half of the 20th century)

Postmodernism means “after modernism.” Artists mixed old and new styles, blurred the line between museum art and popular culture, and asked, “Who decides what counts as art?” Many styles at once is called pluralism.

Who or whatRemember this
Piazza d’Italia (1978)Postmodern plaza in New Orleans with Roman columns made of stainless steel and neon
John Cage, 4′33″ (1952)The pianist plays nothing; the music is every sound in the room
Prepared pianoCage put screws, bolts, and rubber between the strings
Chance musicChoices left to chance, like tossing coins; Merce Cunningham used chance in dance
MinimalismA few simple patterns repeated many times, changing slowly
Terry Riley, Steve Reich, Philip GlassIn C (53 phrases); Clapping Music; Einstein on the Beach
Pop ArtEveryday images from ads and comics in the museum
Andy Warhol and Roy Lichtenstein32 soup-can paintings; giant comic-book frames with Ben-Day dots
Yayoi KusamaPolka dots and mirror rooms that seem to go on forever
Collage, quotation, and samplingMaking something new from pieces of things that already exist
Hip-hopBorn at a 1973 party in the Bronx, where DJ Kool Herc repeated the drum “break”
Arvo PärtSlow, bell-like style called tintinnabuli (“little bells”)
Pauline Oliveros, Meredith Monk, Laurie AndersonDeep Listening; voices used like an orchestra; the tape-bow violin and O Superman
Ellen Taaffe ZwilichFirst woman to win the Pulitzer Prize for Music (1983)
Theatre of the AbsurdBeckett’s Waiting for Godot: Godot never arrives
Postmodern danceJudson Dance Theater made dances from ordinary walking, running, and sitting

21st-century arts and technology

Technology is a tool for making art, a stage for sharing it, and a source of new questions about who the artist is and who owns the work.

Who or whatRemember this
Streaming and algorithmsPlaying music straight from the internet; computer rules that pick your next song
Digital audio workstation (DAW)Software for recording, editing, and mixing music, like GarageBand
Loop and producerA short repeating pattern; the person who shapes how a recording sounds
Billie Eilish and FinneasRecorded her first album in Finneas’s small bedroom
Auto-TunePitch correction that moves a singer’s notes until they are exactly in tune; at its extreme it sounds robotic (T-Pain)
Sampling, remix, mashupBorrowing a short piece of an older recording; a new version of a song; two songs combined
Lil Nas X, “Old Town Road”Beat built from a sample of Nine Inch Nails banjo music; spread on TikTok
CreditNaming who created a song, dance, or artwork, like Jalaiah Harmon’s “Renegade” dance
Eric Whitacre’s Virtual ChoirSingers record alone at home; an editor joins them into one choir
LatencyThe tiny internet delay that makes live singing on video calls fall apart
AI as a repair toolCleaned up John Lennon’s real voice for the Beatles’ “Now and Then”
AI as a creative partnerHolly Herndon’s Holly+, a digital twin of her own voice
AI as a fake copy“Heart on My Sleeve” copied Drake’s and the Weeknd’s voices without asking
Adaptive musicVideo game music that changes as you play
Immersive artArt you step inside, like teamLab Borderless in Tokyo
David HockneyPainted spring arriving at his home on an iPad

Three questions to ask about any use of AI in the arts: Did the artists agree? Were they paid? Who gets the credit?

Practice for the short answers

Each short answer is worth 2 points: 1 point for a correct idea and 1 point for a specific example. Practice each topic below in 2 to 4 complete sentences.

  1. Describe one way Romantic music is different from Classical music, and give an example of a composer, a piece, or a musical feature.

  2. Choose one woman from the unit. What did she create, and what obstacle did she face or what “first” did she achieve?

  3. Choose one big idea. Explain how it appears in one piece of music and in one work of visual art, theater, or dance.

  4. Name two ingredients that helped create jazz in New Orleans, and describe one of them.

  5. Choose one example of AI in music (“Now and Then,” Holly+, or “Heart on My Sleeve”). Is it a repair tool, a creative partner, or a fake copy? Was it fair? Use at least one of the three questions: Did the artists agree? Were they paid? Who gets the credit?
