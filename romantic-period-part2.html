Setting the Scene: Beethoven, the Bridge

🔍 Hotspots: click to uncover hidden facts

An anthem today

The "Ode to Joy" from Beethoven's Ninth Symphony is the official anthem of the European Union.

Conversation books

When he could no longer hear, visitors wrote their questions to Beethoven in notebooks called conversation books.

In Part 1, you saw how Romantic writers, painters, actors, and dancers put feelings first. Musicians did the same. The Romantic period in music lasted from about 1820 to 1900, and it began with one giant figure.

  • Ludwig van Beethoven (1770–1827) stands with one foot in the Classical period and one foot in the Romantic. Born in Bonn, Germany, he moved to Vienna and studied with Haydn.
  • Unlike Haydn, who served a noble family, Beethoven was one of the first freelance composers: he earned his living by selling his music, giving concerts, teaching, and gifts from patrons. That gave him freedom to write music to express himself.
  • He began to lose his hearing in the 1790s and was completely deaf by about 1818, but he kept composing, hearing the music in his mind. In 1802 he wrote a letter to his brothers, the Heiligenstadt Testament, saying that only his art kept him going through his despair. Romantic composers saw him as their great hero.

The Fifth Symphony (1808)

The whole first movement is built from one tiny idea: three short notes and one long note, later called the fate motif, as if fate were knocking at the door. It's passed around the orchestra until it becomes a roar. The symphony begins in a stormy C minor and ends in a triumphant C major, a journey from darkness into light.

Listen to an example

Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, first movement (1808). Count how many times you hear short-short-short-LONG. Notice how it jumps between high and low instruments, and between soft and very loud.

Watch on YouTubeBeethoven: Symphony No. 5, I. Allegro con brio (London Philharmonic Orchestra)
Watch on YouTubeBeethoven's Fifth Symphony: what makes it so captivating? (animated lesson)
Fun fact: Short-short-short-long is the letter V in Morse code. During World War II, the opening of the Fifth Symphony became a symbol of "V for Victory."

🗺 Stop 2 of 9

The Romantic Sound: Longer, Louder, Larger

🔍 Hotspots: click to uncover hidden facts

A new instrument

The tuba was invented in 1835, right in the middle of the Romantic period.

Softer than soft

Tchaikovsky's Sixth Symphony asks the bassoon to play pppppp: six p's!

Romantic composers kept the Classical forms, like the symphony and the concerto, but stretched them in every direction. Remember three words: longer, louder, larger.

Classical (about 1750–1820)Romantic (about 1820–1900)
GoalOrder, balance, and clarity: the headEmotion and self-expression: the heart
MelodyShort, balanced phrases, like an orderly conversationLong, sweeping melodies, like one voice pouring out its feelings
HarmonyStays close to the home keyChromatic harmony, with notes from outside the key, and clashing dissonance
DynamicsFrom pp to ffHuge contrasts, from pppp to ffff
TempoSteadyFlexible, with rubato
LengthA symphony lasts about 25 minutesA symphony lasts about 45 minutes or more
OrchestraAbout 20 to 60 playersClose to 100 players by the end of the century
  • Rubato, Italian for "robbed" time, means slightly holding back or pushing forward the tempo to make the music more expressive.
  • Composers delayed the resolution of dissonance to create feelings of yearning, tension, and mystery.
  • Romantic music explored love, longing, nature, and the fantastic, even witches and the devil.

The growing orchestra

Tone color, the special sound of each instrument, became more important than ever. New instruments joined: the high piccolo, the English horn, the bass clarinet, and the very low contrabassoon; trombones and tubas; and cymbals, the triangle, and the harp. Valves let horns and trumpets play every note of the scale, so the brass could play full melodies. And the piano, with a new cast-iron frame and felt hammers, became louder and more "singing."

Composers, Virtuosos, and Their Public

🔍 Hotspots: click to uncover hidden facts

A deal with the devil?

Paganini played so amazingly that some people whispered he must have made a deal with the devil.

Lind's first concert

Jenny Lind's first American concert, in 1850 at Castle Garden in New York, drew thousands of fans.

Before, composers wrote for a job: Bach for the church, Haydn for a noble family. Romantic composers were free artists who wrote mostly for the growing middle class.

  • Cities founded orchestras and held subscription concerts, where listeners bought tickets for a whole season. The Vienna Philharmonic and the New York Philharmonic both began in 1842.
  • Music schools called conservatories opened in Europe and America. At first, women could only study performance; by the late 1800s, they could study composition too.
  • The piano became a fixture in every middle-class home. Girls, especially, took piano lessons, because playing well was seen as a ladylike skill, even though women were not expected to become professional musicians.
  • Audiences went wild for virtuosos, performers with dazzling technical skill, like the violinist Niccolò Paganini and the pianist Franz Liszt.

Women on the concert stage

Women could become famous as performers, especially as pianists and singers, even when they were discouraged from composing. The pianist Clara Schumann, whom you'll meet soon, toured Europe for 61 years. The Swedish soprano Jenny Lind (1820–1887), nicknamed the "Swedish Nightingale," was so popular that her 1850–1852 tour of the United States, organized by the showman P. T. Barnum, caused "Lind mania." She gave much of her earnings to charity.

A Louisiana connection: Jenny Lind's American tour included concerts in New Orleans in 1851. Crowds packed the theater to hear her.

The Art Song: Schubert and The Erlking

🔍 Hotspots: click to uncover hidden facts

Eight in a day

On one day in October 1815, the teenage Schubert wrote eight songs.

Little mushroom

Schubert's friends nicknamed him "Schwammerl," which means "little mushroom," because he was short and round.

One of the most important Romantic forms is the art song, a composition for solo voice and piano. The piano is an equal partner that helps tell the story. The German word for song is Lied (pronounced "leet"; plural Lieder). A song can be strophic, with the same music for every stanza, or through-composed, with new music for each stanza to follow a changing story.

  • Franz Schubert (1797–1828) of Vienna was the earliest master of the art song. He wrote more than 600 songs!
  • He never had an official job. His friends held parties called Schubertiades where only his music was played.
  • He died at only thirty-one.

The Erlking (1815), which Schubert wrote at eighteen, sets a ballad by Goethe. A father rides through a stormy night holding his sick son, who sees the Erlking, the king of the elves, who stands for death. The song is through-composed, and one singer plays four characters:

CharacterHow Schubert makes it sound
The narratorA middle register, telling the story
The fatherA low register, calm and reassuring
The sonA high register in a minor key; each cry of "My father!" is higher and more frightened
The ErlkingSweet, coaxing melodies in a major key, sung softly

The piano's pounding triplet rhythm suggests the galloping horse. At the end, the gallop stops, and the narrator tells us the child is dead.

Listen to an example

Schubert, Erlkönig (1815). This video shows the English words. Can you hear the son's voice getting higher each time he cries out? Notice that the piano never stops galloping until the very end.

Poets of the Piano: Chopin and Liszt

🔍 Hotspots: click to uncover hidden facts

Few concerts

Chopin gave only about 30 public concerts in his whole life. He much preferred playing for small groups.

A sideways piano

Liszt was among the first pianists to turn the piano sideways on stage, so the audience could see his face and hands.

Frédéric Chopin (1810–1849) grew up in Warsaw, Poland, and moved to Paris at twenty-one. He wrote almost only for the piano, and his melodies make the piano seem to sing. Shy, he preferred small gatherings called salons to big concert halls. His mazurkas and polonaises, Polish dances, show his love for his homeland. A nocturne is a slow, dreamy "night piece"; an étude is a study piece that helps a pianist master a difficult technique.

Listen to an example

Chopin, Nocturne in E-flat Major, Op. 9, No. 2. Listen for the singing melody that returns with fancier and fancier decorations, and for the pianist gently stretching the tempo (rubato).

Watch on YouTubeChopin: Nocturne in E-flat Major, Op. 9, No. 2

Franz Liszt (1811–1886), born in Hungary, heard the violinist Paganini and decided to become "the Paganini of the piano." He practiced eight to twelve hours a day and became the greatest pianist of his time. Audiences went so wild on his tours that the poet Heinrich Heine called it Lisztomania, a bit like fans of a pop star today. Liszt made the solo recital popular and invented a new orchestral form, the symphonic poem. He also taught hundreds of pianists for free.

Listen to an example

Liszt, Transcendental Étude No. 10. Transcendental means going beyond normal limits. Watch the pianist's hands leap across the keyboard!

Watch on YouTubeLiszt: Transcendental Étude No. 10
A Louisiana connection: Louis Moreau Gottschalk (1829–1869), born in New Orleans, sailed to Paris at thirteen to study piano and became the first American concert pianist to gain international fame. He mixed African American, Creole, and Caribbean rhythms into his music, like his piece Bamboula, inspired by dances performed in New Orleans' Congo Square.

Listen to an example

Gottschalk, Bamboula, Op. 2 (1848). Listen for the catchy, repeating dance rhythm, dressed up with sparkling piano runs.

Women Composers: Clara Schumann and Fanny Mendelssohn

🔍 Hotspots: click to uncover hidden facts

On the money

Clara Schumann's portrait appeared on Germany's 100-mark banknote.

A musical calendar

Fanny Hensel wrote Das Jahr (The Year), a set of twelve piano pieces, one for each month.

  • Clara Wieck Schumann (1819–1896) was one of the greatest pianists of the 1800s. Her father, the piano teacher Friedrich Wieck, trained her from early childhood. She was a child prodigy who went on her first concert tour at eleven.
  • In 1840, she married the composer Robert Schumann, even though her father fought the marriage in court. While raising a large family, she kept touring, earned much of the family's income, and introduced Robert's music to the public.
  • She was one of the first pianists to perform from memory, the custom today. Her concert career lasted 61 years, and her close friends included the composer Johannes Brahms.

Clara composed songs, a piano concerto, a piano trio, and piano pieces. But she was told again and again that women could not be great composers, and she began to believe it. She wrote: "A woman must not desire to compose; there has never yet been one able to do it. Should I expect to be the one?" She stopped composing when she was thirty-six. Today, her music is performed and recorded more and more.

Listen to an example

Clara Wieck, Romance in E-flat Minor, Op. 11, No. 1 (1839). Clara wrote this short, lyrical piano piece, called a romance, at nineteen and sent it to Robert. Listen for the mood of sweet sadness, the brighter middle section in major, and the two questioning chords at the end.

Watch on YouTubeClara Schumann: Romance, Op. 11, No. 1 (Isata Kanneh-Mason)
Watch on YouTubeClara Schumann: Music History Crash Course

Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel (1805–1847)

Fanny was the older sister of the famous composer Felix Mendelssohn, and just as talented. But her father told her that music could be Felix's career, while for her it could only be "an ornament." She composed more than 450 pieces anyway, and held concerts in her family's home in Berlin. A few of her songs were first published under Felix's name. When Queen Victoria told Felix that her favorite of "his" songs was Italien, he had to admit that Fanny wrote it! She finally published music under her own name in 1846, the year before she died.

Other women composers include Hildegard of Bingen in the 1100s, and, after the Romantic period, Amy Beach and Florence Price, the first African American woman to have a symphony played by a major American orchestra.

Music That Tells Stories and Celebrates Nations

🔍 Hotspots: click to uncover hidden facts

Brass in the corners

For his huge Requiem, Berlioz placed four extra brass bands around the performers to surround the audience with sound.

Train fan

Dvořák loved trains and enjoyed watching the locomotives at the station.

Program music is instrumental music connected to a story, poem, or scene. The composer tells us the subject with a title or notes called the program.

Hector Berlioz (1803–1869) fell madly in love with the actress Harriet Smithson, and wrote the Symphonie fantastique (1830) about his hopeless love. In this five-movement story, the beloved becomes a melody called the idée fixe ("fixed idea") that appears in every movement, changed each time: a graceful waltz at a ball, and finally a shrill, grotesque dance at a witches' gathering. (Harriet later heard the symphony and married him!)

Listen to an example

Meet the idée fixe. Leonard Bernstein plays the idée fixe and shows how it rises, strains, and collapses, like a lovesick person who can't stop thinking about someone.

Watch on YouTubeBerlioz Takes a Trip: the idée fixe (Leonard Bernstein)

Music for the homeland

Nationalism, a strong love of one's homeland, inspired composers to use folk songs, dances, and legends. Bedřich Smetana wrote The Moldau (1874) about the main river of his Czech homeland, flowing from two tiny springs past forests, a peasant wedding, and moonlit water nymphs to the city of Prague. Antonín Dvořák came to New York in 1892 and, inspired by African American spirituals he learned from his student Henry T. Burleigh, wrote the New World Symphony (1893). In Russia, Tchaikovsky wrote the music for the ballets Swan Lake and The Nutcracker.

Listen to an example

Smetana, The Moldau (1874). Listen for two flutes playing the little springs, the broad river theme in the violins, horns sounding a hunt, and a polka for the peasant wedding.

Watch on YouTubeSmetana: Vltava (The Moldau) (Berliner Philharmoniker)

Opera: The Biggest Show of the Century

🔍 Hotspots: click to uncover hidden facts

Opera in Egypt

Verdi's Aida, set in ancient Egypt, premiered in Cairo in 1871.

A very long story

Wagner's Ring cycle takes about 15 hours to perform, over four evenings.

Before movies and TV, opera, a story told through music and singing, was one of the most popular entertainments. The words are the libretto. A solo song about a character's feelings is an aria, and the highest female voice is the soprano.

  • Giuseppe Verdi (1813–1901) of Italy is the most popular opera composer of all. His chorus "Va, pensiero" from Nabucco (1842), sung by exiles longing for home, became a song of Italian independence.
  • Richard Wagner (1813–1883) of Germany wrote enormous music dramas based on myths, like the four-part Ring cycle. He used leitmotifs, short musical ideas connected with a character or object, like the themes for heroes and villains in movies today. Wagner also wrote hateful essays against Jewish people, which is why his legacy is still debated.
  • Giacomo Puccini (1858–1924) wrote La Bohème (1896), about poor young artists in Paris, an example of verismo ("realism"). The Broadway musical Rent retells its story.

Listen to an example

Wagner, "Ride of the Valkyries" from Die Walküre. The Valkyries, warrior daughters of the king of the gods, ride through the sky. Listen for the galloping rhythm and the heroic leitmotif blasted by the brass.

Watch on YouTube"The Ride of the Valkyries" (Vienna Philharmonic)

Women of the opera stage

Opera gave women some of the most powerful roles on any stage. Star sopranos and mezzo-sopranos were among the most famous and best-paid performers in the world. Bizet's Carmen (1875) features a bold, free-spirited heroine who lives by her own rules, so rebellious that the first audiences were shocked. Carmen is a famous role for a mezzo-soprano, a voice lower than a soprano.

A Louisiana connection: New Orleans was one of America's opera capitals. The French Opera House opened on Bourbon Street in 1859 with Rossini's William Tell, and for sixty years it was the center of the city's social life, until it burned down in 1919. Several operas had their first American performances in New Orleans.
