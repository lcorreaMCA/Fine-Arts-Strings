    Room 1

    Introduction to the Baroque

    🔒 Room 1 is locked. Solve the lock at the end of Room 0 to open it.

    The Baroque era lasted from about 1600 to 1750. Composers took the musical traditions of the Renaissance and expanded them: music became more complex, ensembles grew larger, and Italian opera brought new drama to music all over Europe. Wealthy patrons (people who pay artists to create work) supported the arts, and more people came to accept instrumental music that wasn’t written for church, which led to lots of new ideas and experiments.

    Where does the name come from?

    Baroque comes from a Portuguese word for an irregularly shaped pearl used in jewelry. When people first used the word in the early 1700s, it was an insult! Today it connects the music to the grand, over-the-top art and architecture of the time, like the Palace of Versailles in France and the richly decorated St. Peter’s Basilica in Vatican City.

    Italy leads the way

    Italy was the biggest source of inspiration for Baroque composers. The Italian words musicians still use today, like forte (loud) and allegro (fast), became popular during this time.

    Four key features of Baroque music

    • Counterpoint: several independent melodies woven together, a technique carried over from Renaissance polyphony (music with many melody lines at once).
    • Basso continuo: a steady bass line with chords that gives the music a reliable foundation for the harmony and melody above it.
    • Figured bass: a kind of musical shorthand, with small numbers written under the bass notes to tell the keyboard player which chords to play. It works a lot like the chord symbols jazz and pop players read today.
    • Ornamentation: decorating a melody with extra notes, from simple trills and turns to fancy scales and broken chords.

    Explore: How a basso continuo is built

    Click each step in order.

    The composer writes only a bass line. A cello (or bassoon) plays it: this is the foundation.

    Small numbers, called figures, are written under the bass notes. Each number is a hint about which chord fits there.

    The harpsichord or organ player reads the bass with the left hand and makes up chords with the right hand, following the figures.

    Together they make the basso continuo: a steady bass plus chords under the melody. Every performance can sound a little different, because the keyboard player improvises.

    Baroque pieces also keep a unity of mood: one basic feeling, such as joy, grief, or excitement, lasts through a whole movement. At the time, these feelings were called “the affections.”

    Other big changes

    • Virtuosity: music became harder to play, and audiences loved watching skilled performers show off. A performer with amazing technical skill is a virtuoso.
    • Major and minor keys: composers organized music around major and minor keys, a system still used today.
    • New and improved instruments: in Cremona, Italy, Antonio Stradivari set new standards for building violins. The Italian instrument maker Bartolomeo Cristofori invented the pianoforte around 1700, the instrument we now simply call the piano!

    Pioneers of the Baroque

    Alessandro Scarlatti helped shape the form of opera. Johann Jakob Froberger made the four-movement dance suite a standard form for keyboard music. Antonio Vivaldi set the standard for writing and performing concertos. George Frideric Handel and Johann Sebastian Bach became the most famous Baroque composers of all, and their music still inspires listeners around the world.

    ⚜️ Louisiana connection

    The oldest known piece of written music in Louisiana is a Baroque songbook. In 1754, the Ursuline nuns of New Orleans, who had arrived in 1727 and started a school for girls, received a hand-copied book of about 300 songs, copied in Paris in 1736. It includes tunes by Lully and Couperin, written just as this room describes: one melody line plus a figured bass, so the nuns and their students could fill in the harmony themselves. The original is kept today by the Historic New Orleans Collection.

    Room 2

    Baroque Forms

    🔒 Room 2 is locked. Solve the lock at the end of Room 1 to open it.

    A form is the type or plan of a piece of music, the way a building has a blueprint. A movement is one complete section of a longer piece, with a short pause before the next.

    Concertos: soloists vs. the group

    Baroque composers loved the contrast between a few players and many players. Two kinds of concertos grew out of this idea:

    • Concerto grosso: a small group of soloists, usually two to four players, plays against a larger group called the tutti (Italian for “all”).
    • Solo concerto: one soloist plays with an orchestra.

    Both usually have three movements: fast, slow, fast.

    Ritornello form

    The fast movements of concertos often use ritornello form. The ritornello (Italian for “refrain”) is a theme played by the whole orchestra. It keeps coming back, often in shorter pieces and different keys, with solo sections in between. It works a lot like the chorus of a song returning between the verses!

    Explore: Ritornello form

    Click each block from left to right, and watch the tension meter rise and fall.

    Tension

    Tutti (ritornello): the whole orchestra plays the main theme. This is “home.” Tension is low.

    Solo: the soloist takes off with new, flashy music. We wonder when the theme will come back.

    Tutti: part of the ritornello returns, often in a new key. A short rest for the ear.

    Solo: the soloist goes further, with harder and faster passages. The tension climbs.

    Tutti: another piece of the ritornello, sometimes in a minor key for drama.

    Solo: the most brilliant solo of all. This is the peak.

    Tutti (full ritornello): the whole theme returns in the home key. We’re back home, and the tension melts away.

    Other Baroque forms

    FormWhat it is
    Trio sonataA piece with three musical lines, two melodies plus a basso continuo, but played by four musicians, because the continuo needs a keyboard and a low instrument
    FugueA piece built on one main theme, the subject, which each voice imitates in turn, like a very clever round
    SuiteA set of movements based on dances, all in the same key. The standard four are the allemande, courante, sarabande, and gigue, a pattern made popular by Johann Jakob Froberger
    OperaA play in which the story is sung, with arias (songs expressing feelings) and recitatives (speech-like singing that moves the story forward)
    CantataA sung piece for church, with chorus, soloists, and orchestra, like a sermon in music
    OratorioA large sung work, usually on a Bible story, performed without acting, scenery, or costumes

    Each dance in a suite has its own personality: the allemande (from Germany) is moderate and flowing, the courante is lively, the sarabande (from Spain) is slow and dignified, and the gigue (the jig, from England and Ireland) is fast and bouncy.

    Room 3

    Early Baroque Composers

    🔒 Room 3 is locked. Solve the lock at the end of Room 2 to open it.

    Claudio Monteverdi (1567–1643)

    Claudio Monteverdi was born in Cremona, Italy, the town famous for violin making. He wrote Orfeo (1607), considered the first great opera, for the court of Mantua. He later became music director at St. Mark’s, the most important church in Venice, and wrote operas for Venice’s new public opera houses.

    He wanted music to express strong emotions like anger, excitement, and passion. He was one of the first composers to write two string techniques into music: pizzicato (plucking the strings) and tremolo (very fast repeated bowing). Both are still used today.

    In Orfeo, the musician Orpheus learns that his wife, Eurydice, has died from a snakebite. In the recitative Tu se’ morta (“You are dead”), Monteverdi uses word painting, music that acts out the meaning of the words: the word stelle (stars) is sung on a high note, and abissi (the deep pits of the underworld) on a low one.

    Henry Purcell (about 1659–1695)

    Henry Purcell is often called the greatest English composer. He was a choirboy in the king’s chapel and later the organist of Westminster Abbey, and he wrote music for church, for the royal court, and for London’s theaters. His opera Dido and Aeneas (1689) had its first known performance at a girls’ boarding school in London. It lasts only about an hour and uses just strings and harpsichord.

    Its most famous song, Dido’s Lament, is built on a ground bass: a bass pattern repeated over and over while the melody changes above it. In the story, Queen Dido is heartbroken when the hero Aeneas sails away, and she sings this farewell before she dies.

    Explore: Inside Dido’s Lament

    The ground bass repeats 11 times. Click through some of the repeats and watch the tension rise toward “Remember me!”

    Tension

    The lute and strings play the ground bass alone: a bass line that slides slowly downward, one half step at a time. A descending, chromatic line like this meant grief in Baroque music.

    Dido begins to sing “When I am laid in earth.” Her melody moves freely above the bass.

    Her melody and the bass rub against each other, making painful dissonances (notes that clash).

    “Remember me!” Dido reaches the highest note of the aria.

    “Remember me, but ah! forget my fate.” The melody sinks back down.

    After the last of the bass’s 11 repeats, the violins take over with a sad melody that slides downward, like the bass did.

    Arcangelo Corelli (1653–1713)

    Arcangelo Corelli was the most famous Italian violinist around 1700. He wrote only music for strings, about sixty sonatas and twelve concertos, and he was a great teacher who helped lay the foundations of modern violin technique.

    Women composers

    During the Baroque period, women were not allowed to be music directors or to play in court and opera orchestras. Even so, several women became respected composers, including Francesca Caccini in Florence, Barbara Strozzi in Venice, and Élisabeth Jacquet de La Guerre in Paris, who played harpsichord for Louis XIV.

    Room 4

    Spotlight on Antonio Vivaldi

    🔒 Room 4 is locked. Solve the lock at the end of Room 3 to open it.

    The Red Priest

    Antonio Vivaldi was born on March 4, 1678, in Venice, Italy. His father, a baker who became a professional violinist, gave him his first music lessons. Vivaldi trained to be a priest, and because of his bright red hair, he was nicknamed “the Red Priest.”

    The Pietà

    In 1703, Vivaldi began teaching at the Pietà, a music school in Venice for girls, most of whom were orphans. He worked there on and off until 1740 as a violin teacher, composer, and conductor. The girls’ orchestra was one of the finest in Italy, and Vivaldi wrote many pieces for them. On Sundays and holidays they performed in the chapel from a balcony, hidden behind an iron screen, so listeners heard them without seeing them.

    King of the concerto

    Vivaldi was a thrilling violinist and wrote about 450 concertos for many instruments, including violin, cello, flute, oboe, and even 37 for bassoon. Bach admired Vivaldi’s music so much that he arranged some of his concertos for keyboard.

    🔍 Fact check

    The video says Vivaldi died “at the age of 67.” He was born in March 1678 and died in July 1741, so he was 63. The video also lists the harp among his concerto instruments; his unusual choices were really instruments like the mandolin and the lute.

    Forgotten and found

    In 1741, Vivaldi traveled to Vienna, where he died a month later, poor and nearly forgotten. His music was mostly ignored for about 200 years, until a Baroque revival in the 1950s, helped by the new long-playing records, made him famous again.

    🔗 Connection

    While Vivaldi was writing his violin music, Antonio Stradivari was building violins in Cremona, Monteverdi’s hometown. His violins were perfect for the brilliant solo music of Baroque concertos, and today some sell for millions of dollars. Think about it: why might a 300-year-old violin be worth so much more than a new one?

    Room 5

    The Four Seasons

    🔒 Room 5 is locked. Solve the lock at the end of Room 4 to open it.

    Vivaldi’s most famous work is The Four Seasons (1725), four violin concertos called Spring, Summer, Autumn, and Winter. You’ve probably heard it in movies and commercials! Each concerto came with a poem describing what the music shows.

    Music that tells a story or paints a scene is called program music, and Vivaldi was generations ahead of his time. He even wrote lines from the poems into the score, along with labels like “the sleeping goatherd” and “the barking dog.”

    SeasonWhat you’ll hear
    SpringBirds singing, streams flowing, a thunderstorm, a sleeping goatherd with his barking dog, and a country dance
    SummerA turtle dove singing, then a hailstorm flattening the fields
    AutumnHunters dashing out after their prey
    WinterTeeth chattering in the cold, a crackling fire, and slipping on the ice

    Spring, first movement: a listening map

    The first movement of Spring is in ritornello form. The cheerful spring theme keeps returning, and between its returns the violins imitate birds with high trills and paint the thunderstorm with fast, rushing notes. Each phrase of the theme is played loudly and then repeated softly, like an echo: terraced dynamics.

    🎧 Listen to an example: Vivaldi, Spring, first movement (Allegro)

    Open the recording on YouTube

    Follow the map and check each moment you hear.

    1. Ritornello: the whole orchestra plays the cheerful spring theme, loud, then soft like an echo.
    2. Birds: the solo violin and two violins from the orchestra trill and chirp up high.
    3. Ritornello: part of the theme returns.
    4. Murmuring streams: soft, running notes in the violins.
    5. Ritornello again.
    6. Thunder and lightning: shivering tremolos and rushing scales; the solo violin flashes like lightning.
    7. Ritornello in a minor key: the storm has darkened the mood.
    8. Birds return: trills and chirps after the storm.
    9. Final ritornello: the spring theme returns in the home key, loud then soft.

    Second movement: the sleeping goatherd

    In the slow second movement, the solo violin sings the goatherd’s peaceful dream, the other violins rustle like leaves, and the violas imitate his dog’s bark with a repeated “woof-woof” rhythm. Vivaldi marked the viola part very loud and rough. The violinist and conductor Jeanne Lamon found that if the violas really played that loud, you couldn’t hear anything else, so she asked them to play strongly, but not quite as loud. Performers still argue about how much bark to give it!

    Room 6

    Johann Sebastian Bach

    🔒 Room 6 is locked. Solve the lock at the end of Room 5 to open it.

    One of the greatest composers of all time

    Bach (1685–1750) was born in Eisenach, Germany, into a family of musicians. He wrote more than 1,000 pieces in almost every style of his time except opera, including music for organ and harpsichord. The piano had only just been invented and was still rare. He was a master of counterpoint: music where several independent melodies play at the same time and fit together perfectly.

    A working musician

    Bach wrote music for whatever job he had: organ music as a church organist, concertos as a court music director, and church cantatas as music director for four churches in Leipzig. Musicians then didn’t have much freedom. When Bach tried to leave his job in Weimar for a new one, the Duke of Weimar put him in jail for a month!

    In Leipzig, where he spent the last 27 years of his life, Bach wrote a new cantata for almost every Sunday and taught at the St. Thomas school. On Friday nights he led a student orchestra in a coffeehouse. Bach was a devoted Lutheran who often wrote “S.D.G.” at the end of his music, short for the Latin Soli Deo Gloria, “Glory to God alone.”

    Famous works

    • The Well-Tempered Clavier: 48 preludes and fugues in every major and minor key. Listen: Prelude and Fugue No. 1
    • Six suites for solo cello: melodies so cleverly written that they suggest harmony with no other instrument needed. Listen: Suite No. 1, Prelude
    • Brandenburg Concertos: six concertos with different groups of instruments. No. 3 is written for three violins, three violas, and three cellos, plus continuo, so every string section gets the spotlight. Listen: Brandenburg Concerto No. 3
    • The Art of Fugue: his unfinished final work, which includes a theme spelling his own name, B–A–C–H, as musical notes. (In German, B means B-flat and H means B-natural.) Listen: Contrapunctus 1, played by a string quartet
    • Cantatas and Passions: more than 200 cantatas, plus the St. Matthew Passion and St. John Passion, which tell the story of the last days of Jesus. A cantata often ends with a chorale, a simple hymn tune the whole church congregation knew. Listen: Cantata No. 140 Listen: St. Matthew Passion

    Explore: How a fugue works

    Click through the parts of a fugue. The tension meter shows how the excitement builds.

    Tension

    The subject (main theme) is played alone in the top voice. Remember it!

    A second voice plays the subject a little higher or lower (the “answer”), while the first voice keeps going with a countersubject, a partner melody.

    A third voice enters. Now three melodies weave together: counterpoint.

    The lowest voice enters. On the organ, the feet play it on the pedals. All voices are now in.

    An episode: a passage without the full subject, often with patterns repeated higher or lower. It travels to new keys.

    Stretto: voices start the subject before the last one has finished, crowding together. Exciting!

    The subject one last time, often deep in the bass, then a strong final chord.

    Lost and found

    In his own time, Bach was known mainly as an amazing organist, not as a great composer. After his death, his music was mostly forgotten until the 1800s. In 1829, the composer Felix Mendelssohn conducted the St. Matthew Passion, and Bach’s music came back. Today, many consider Bach the greatest composer who ever lived, because his music is both emotional and brilliantly constructed.

    Room 7

    George Frideric Handel

    🔒 Room 7 is locked. Solve the lock at the end of Room 6 to open it.

    Handel (1685–1759) was born in Halle, Germany, just one month before Bach. His father wanted him to study law, but his talent was too great to ignore. He worked in Hamburg and Italy, then moved to London, where he became England’s most important composer and wrote 39 Italian operas. When opera stopped making money, he turned to English oratorios: large sung works, usually on a Bible story, performed without acting, scenery, or costumes.

    Handel’s German employer once gave him permission to visit England for a “reasonable time.” He stayed for the rest of his life, almost 50 years!

    Messiah (1741)

    Handel’s most famous work is the oratorio Messiah, which he composed in just 24 days! It lasts about two and a half hours.

    It premiered in Dublin, Ireland, in 1742 to raise money for people in debtors’ prisons. So many people wanted to attend that women were asked not to wear hoopskirts, and men were asked to leave their swords at home, to fit more people in!

    Listen for word painting

    In “Ev’ry Valley,” Handel uses amazing word painting. The word “exalted” (raised up) climbs upward on 46 quick notes! “Mountain” is sung high, “low” is sung low, “crooked” wiggles back and forth, and “plain” (meaning flat and smooth) is one long, level note.

    The “Hallelujah” Chorus: a listening map

    The “Hallelujah” Chorus, which ends Part II of Messiah, is one of the most famous choral pieces in the world. Handel keeps it exciting by switching among three textures, the ways musical lines combine: monophonic (one melody, everyone in unison), homophonic (one melody with chords, everyone moving together), and polyphonic (several independent melodies at once).

    🎧 Listen to an example: Handel, “Hallelujah” Chorus

    Open the recording on YouTube

    1. Short shouts of “Hallelujah!” with everyone singing the same rhythm together (homophonic).
    2. “For the Lord God omnipotent reigneth”: all voices sing the same melody in unison, full and powerful.
    3. The long, strong melody is set against quick shouts of “Hallelujah!” Now the parts move independently (polyphonic).
    4. “The kingdom of this world” is sung softly and smoothly, like a hymn.
    5. “And He shall reign for ever and ever”: the basses start, and the other voices enter one by one in imitation.
    6. “King of Kings, and Lord of Lords”: long repeated notes climb higher and higher against “Hallelujah!”
    7. A dramatic pause, then the final, slow “Hallelujah!”

    Audiences often stand during the “Hallelujah” Chorus. A famous story says King George II stood up at a London performance, so everyone else had to stand too, but historians aren’t sure it really happened.
