Setting the scene

In Part 1, you learned the building blocks of the Classical style: balanced phrases, the standard orchestra, and forms like sonata form, minuet and trio, theme and variations, and rondo. Now we'll hear how the three great masters, Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven, used them.

These years were full of dramatic political change. Power and money shifted from the nobles to the middle class, and so did the careers of composers. All three masters worked in Vienna, one of Europe's great music capitals.

The French Revolution (1789–1799)

By 1789, France was nearly bankrupt, and ordinary people were tired of paying heavy taxes while the king, nobles, and clergy lived in luxury. On July 14, 1789, a crowd in Paris stormed the Bastille, a royal prison and symbol of the king's power. The new National Assembly issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which said that people are born free and equal in rights. In 1792, France ended its monarchy and became a republic, and King Louis XVI was executed the next year. Years of violence and war followed, until the general Napoleon Bonaparte took power in 1799.

The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)

Saint-Domingue, a French colony in the Caribbean, was the richest colony in the world. Its sugar and coffee were produced by hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans, who were treated with terrible cruelty. Inspired by the ideas of liberty and equality, the enslaved people rose up in 1791. Their great leader, Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man, became a brilliant general. France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1794. When Napoleon sent an army to retake the colony, Toussaint was captured and died in a French prison, but his fellow general Jean-Jacques Dessalines led the fight to victory. On January 1, 1804, he declared independence. Haiti became the first independent Black republic, and the first country founded by people who had freed themselves from slavery.

Connection to Louisiana: The Haitian Revolution changed our own history. After losing Saint-Domingue, Napoleon no longer needed France's North American lands, so in 1803 he sold the Louisiana Territory to the United States: the Louisiana Purchase. And in 1809, thousands of refugees from Saint-Domingue arrived in New Orleans, nearly doubling the city's population and helping shape its music, food, and culture.

The revolutions and the arts

  • Music: revolutionary France held huge outdoor festivals with massive choirs and bands. La Marseillaise (1792) became France's national anthem, and the Paris Conservatory, a public music school, opened in 1795. Beethoven's only opera, Fidelio, tells the story of a wife who rescues her husband, a political prisoner, from an unjust jail, celebrating freedom.
  • Art: the painter Jacques-Louis David took part in the French Revolution and later painted Napoleon.
  • Dance: the ballet master Noverre fled to London during the Revolution.

Art: Neoclassicism and the First Romantics

By the 1780s, Neoclassicism had replaced the Rococo. The leading painter was Jacques-Louis David, who took part in the French Revolution. He painted scenes from ancient history with firm lines, clear light, and serious subjects to inspire courage and patriotism.

Neoclassicism shaped architecture too. Monticello, Thomas Jefferson's home in Virginia, uses columns and a dome inspired by ancient Greek and Roman buildings. Meanwhile, the Spanish painter Francisco Goya used his art to protest cruelty and war. His painting The Third of May, 1808 (painted in 1814) shows the cruelty of Napoleon's soldiers in Spain. Goya was one of the first artists of Romanticism, a movement that valued strong emotion and individual expression.

Connection to music: Beethoven admired Napoleon as a hero of freedom and planned to name his Third Symphony "Bonaparte." When Napoleon declared himself emperor, Beethoven crossed out the name and later called it the Eroica: "Heroic Symphony composed to celebrate the memory of a great man." Like Goya, Beethoven's music led the way from Classical balance toward Romantic emotion.

The Big Three Genres: Symphony, Concerto, and Chamber Music

The Classical symphony

The symphony was the Classical period's great contribution to orchestral music. Haydn wrote at least 104 symphonies, Mozart over 40, and Beethoven 9. A symphony is a long, ambitious work for orchestra, usually lasting 20 to 45 minutes, in four movements:

  1. Fast: vigorous and dramatic, almost always in sonata form, sometimes with a slow introduction
  2. Slow: lyrical and songlike, usually in a different key from the other movements
  3. Dance: a minuet and trio (Beethoven preferred a fast, energetic scherzo)
  4. Fast: lively and brilliant, often in sonata form or sonata-rondo form

Each movement usually has its own themes, but the movements balance and complete each other. Haydn and Mozart wrote many symphonies because their employers needed a steady flow of music for palace concerts. Beethoven wrote a symphony only when he felt inspired, and his were longer and meant for large concert halls.

The Classical concerto

A concerto is a three-movement work for a soloist and orchestra: fast, slow, fast, with no minuet. The soloist is the star, but soloist and orchestra are equally important, trading melodies back and forth. Mozart and Beethoven, the greatest masters of the Classical concerto, often wrote concertos to play themselves, and the piano was their favorite solo instrument.

  • Cadenza: near the end of the first movement, the orchestra pauses on a tense chord, marked with a fermata (𝄐), meaning "pause." Then the soloist plays alone, showing off with dazzling scales and broken chords. A long trill signals the orchestra to come back in. In the Classical era, soloists often improvised their cadenzas.
  • Double exposition: the first movement has two expositions, one for the orchestra and one starting with the soloist's dramatic entrance.

Classical chamber music

Chamber music is written for a small group, two to nine musicians with one player on each part, to be played in a room (a "chamber") in a home or palace. It was fashionable for nobles and wealthy middle-class families to play chamber music with friends after dinner.

  • There's no conductor: each player listens closely and works as a team, a bit like a small jazz group.
  • The most important form is the string quartet: two violins, a viola, and a cello, usually in four movements like a symphony. It's often compared to a lively conversation among four intelligent people.
  • Other popular groups: the sonata for violin and piano, the piano trio (violin, cello, piano), and the string quintet (two violins, two violas, cello).

Joseph Haydn: A Servant Becomes a Celebrity

In Part 1, you met Haydn as a servant of the Esterházy family. By 1790, his music was famous across Europe. In 1791–1792 and again in 1794–1795, he traveled to London, where his concerts were triumphs. The twelve symphonies he wrote for these visits are known as the London Symphonies. A servant had become a celebrity: he was celebrated by the nobility, given an honorary doctorate by Oxford University, and received by the royal family.

Back in Vienna, rich and honored, he wrote six masses and two great oratorios, The Creation (1798) and The Seasons (1801). They were so popular that choruses and orchestras were formed just to perform them. He died in 1809, at age 77.

Haydn's music

  • Haydn was a pathfinder of the Classical style and a pioneer of the symphony and the string quartet. Both Mozart and Beethoven were influenced by him.
  • His music is robust, direct, and full of optimism, often with a folk flavor.
  • He loved humor, with comic surprises from unexpected pauses and sudden changes in dynamics.
  • He wrote 104 symphonies and 68 string quartets. Many symphonies have nicknames: Surprise (No. 94), Military (No. 100), Clock (No. 101), and Drum Roll (No. 103).
  • He was a great innovator who disliked rigid "rules." He believed the educated ear, not a rulebook, should decide what sounds right.

Listen: Trumpet Concerto in E-flat Major (1796)

Haydn wrote this concerto for a friend, a trumpeter at the Vienna court who had invented a keyed trumpet that could play every note of the scale. The concerto was then forgotten for almost 130 years, until it was published in 1929 and recorded in the 1930s. Today it may be Haydn's most popular work, played on the modern valve trumpet. Its third movement is a dazzling sonata-rondo: A B A B′ A, development, A″, coda.

Listen to it two ways: on a keyed trumpet like the one Haydn wrote for, and on a modern trumpet played by Wynton Marsalis, the famous trumpeter from New Orleans. Can you hear the difference in tone color?

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

  • A child prodigy: Mozart (1756–1791) was born in Salzburg, Austria. By age 6 he played harpsichord and violin, improvised, and read music at first sight. He wrote a symphony at 8, an oratorio at 11, and an opera at 12.
  • On tour: his father, Leopold, a court musician, took him on tour from ages 6 to 15. He played for Empress Maria Theresa in Vienna, King Louis XV at Versailles, and King George III in London.
  • Treated like a servant: back in Salzburg, the ruling prince-archbishop didn't appreciate his music and gave him only a low position. Mozart had been an international star as a boy and couldn't stand it.
  • Freelance in Vienna: at 25, he broke free and moved to Vienna. His first years were a success: his opera The Abduction from the Seraglio (1782) was praised, the emperor and nobles came to his concerts, and pupils paid high fees. Haydn told Mozart's father that his son was the greatest composer he knew.

Operas and hard times: The Marriage of Figaro (1786) was loved in Vienna and even more in Prague, which then asked him for Don Giovanni (1787). But Vienna's taste was fickle, and many listeners found his music too complicated. His popularity faded, his pupils dwindled, and he fell into debt.

His last year: in 1791, Mozart wrote the German comic opera The Magic Flute, a great success. A stranger also brought him an anonymous request to write a requiem, a mass for the dead. (The stranger was a servant of a nobleman who planned to pretend he had written it himself.) Mozart died on December 5, 1791, shortly before his 36th birthday, leaving the requiem unfinished. His student Franz Süssmayr completed it.

What does "K." mean? Mozart wrote more than 600 works. In the 1800s, Ludwig von Köchel listed them all in order, so we call each one by its "K." number, like Symphony No. 40, K. 550.

Mozart's music

  • Mozart was one of the most versatile composers ever, writing masterpieces in every form of his time: symphonies, string quartets, piano concertos, and operas.
  • His music sounds effortless, full of ease, grace, balance, and perfect proportion, with darker moments for contrast. He wrote his last three symphonies in only six weeks!
  • His piano concertos, mostly written for his own performances, are among his greatest works.
  • He was a master of opera, with a keen sense of theater and a gift for creating characters through music. His three great Italian comic operas, including The Marriage of Figaro and Don Giovanni, have librettos by Lorenzo da Ponte. His finest German opera is The Magic Flute. His characters feel like real people who think, feel, and change.
Connection to theater: Mozart's operas belong to the opera buffa tradition, comic opera about everyday people that often poked fun at society, just like the comedies the middle class loved. Don Giovanni mixes comedy with seriousness and the supernatural: at the end, a ghostly statue comes to life.

Listen: Eine kleine Nachtmusik, third movement (1787)

Eine kleine Nachtmusik means "A Little Night Music." It's a serenade, light music for evening entertainment, written for a small string orchestra or a string quartet plus double bass. The third movement is a courtly minuet and trio in A B A form.

Listen: Symphony No. 40 in G Minor, K. 550, first movement (1788)

Symphony No. 40 is Mozart's most passionate and dramatic symphony. Its first movement is a perfect example of sonata form. It's built on a three-note motive with the rhythm short-short-long, heard right at the start. Click through each section.

Explore

Sonata form in Mozart's Symphony No. 40

Musical tension

First theme (G minor): the violins play the main theme softly over a throbbing accompaniment in the violas. The short-short-long rhythm gives a feeling of urgency. Bridge: a new staccato motive, played loudly. Second theme (B-flat major): lyrical and gentle, passed between strings and woodwinds. Closing section: the three-note motive sounds gentle and sad, passed between clarinet and bassoon over sighing strings. Then the whole exposition repeats.

The music becomes feverish. The opening theme moves through different keys and is cut into smaller and smaller pieces. A sudden burst of polyphony adds excitement, and finally only the three-note motive is left. The tension is solved only when the whole opening theme returns in the home key.

The themes return, all in the home key of G minor. The bridge becomes even more dramatic, and the lyrical second theme, now in minor instead of major, sounds touching and sad.

The main-theme motive returns softly in the strings, and the full orchestra ends the movement loudly, in minor.

Watch on YouTubeMozart: Symphony No. 40, I. Molto allegro (Norwegian Chamber Orchestra)

The rest of the symphony: a gentle, longing slow movement (the only one in major), a fierce minuet in G minor with a gentler trio in major, and a very fast, tense finale in sonata form. Its passion looks ahead to the Romantic style.

Mozart's Piano Concerto No. 23 in A Major, K. 488 (1786) shows his other side: graceful and poetic, with a gently sad slow movement. Mozart kept it, he said, for a small circle of music lovers, and today it's one of his best-known piano concertos. Listen for the double exposition in the first movement, with the orchestra first and then the piano.

Ludwig van Beethoven: The Bridge to Romanticism

  • Early life: Beethoven (1770–1827) was born in Bonn, Germany, into a family of musicians. By 11, he was assistant to the court organist, and at 12 he had piano pieces published. At 16 he played for Mozart, who reportedly told people to keep their eyes on him.
  • Vienna: shortly before turning 22, he moved to Vienna to study with Haydn and stayed for the rest of his life. Vienna was dazzled by his piano playing and his improvisations.
  • An independent artist: Beethoven insisted that an artist deserved as much respect as a noble. Unlike Haydn and Mozart, he never served a patron. In 1809, three noblemen promised him a yearly income just to keep him in Vienna, something that had never happened before. He also earned money from lessons, concerts, and publishers.

Deafness and heroism

When he was about 29, Beethoven noticed the first signs of deafness, and doctors couldn't stop it. In 1802, he wrote a heartfelt letter to his brothers, now called the Heiligenstadt Testament, about his despair. In it, he said his art gave him the strength to keep going, because he still had so much music to create. His victory over despair matched a change in his music: after this crisis, his works had a new power and heroism, beginning with the gigantic Eroica Symphony (1803).

As his hearing faded, he stopped performing as a pianist by about age 44, though he kept conducting. Friends talked to him through an ear trumpet and, in his last years, by writing in notebooks. He loved walking in the countryside, and his Sixth Symphony, the Pastoral, captures life in the country. Even totally deaf, he wrote some of his greatest works, including the Ninth Symphony.

Beethoven's music

  • For Beethoven, music was not just entertainment but a moral force. He worked hard on every piece, carrying sketchbooks everywhere and revising ideas again and again.
  • He used Classical forms but gave them new power and intensity: bigger contrasts, stronger accents, wider dynamics, and longer developments and codas. He's the bridge between the Classical and Romantic eras.
  • He liked to connect movements, sometimes leading straight from one to the next, and often built toward a triumphant finale.
  • He replaced the minuet with the energetic scherzo.
  • His works: 9 symphonies, 32 piano sonatas, 16 string quartets, 5 piano concertos, two masses, and one opera, Fidelio.
  • His odd-numbered symphonies tend to be forceful, and the even-numbered ones calmer and more lyrical.

Listen: Pathétique Sonata, first movement (1798)

The title Pathétique (meaning full of deep, tragic feeling) was given by the publisher, and Beethoven liked it. The piano sonata opens with a slow, solemn introduction in C minor, built on a dotted rhythm, with crashing chords, sudden contrasts, and dramatic pauses. Then a breathless, very fast movement in sonata form takes over. The slow introduction even returns as a surprise in the development and the coda.

Listen: Symphony No. 5 in C Minor (1808)

The Fifth Symphony opens with one of the most famous ideas in all music: short-short-short-long. Beethoven reportedly described it as "fate knocking at the door." The whole symphony moves from struggle in C minor to victory in C major, and the four-note rhythm comes back in other movements to tie them together.

Listening map

Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, 1st movement: Allegro con brio

  1. Exposition: first themeAll the strings hammer out the four-note motive twice, the second time a step lower. The motive keeps driving forward, passed between instruments in C minor.
  2. Exposition: second themeA horn call announces a calm, legato melody in E-flat major, but the four-note rhythm keeps muttering in the cellos and basses.
  3. DevelopmentThe horn call is broken into smaller pieces until only a single tone is left, echoed softly between woodwinds and strings.
  4. RecapitulationThe full orchestra thunders the motive. A surprise: a short, expressive oboe solo.
  5. CodaA long, exciting coda in C minor, like a second development, builds even more power.

Theater: Weimar Classicism and Romanticism

After the emotional storms of Sturm und Drang, German theater found a new balance, just as Classical music did.

Weimar Classicism

  • Came after Sturm und Drang
  • Tried to balance emotions with reason
  • Wanted to create beautiful and meaningful art
  • Key figures: Johann Wolfgang von Goethe and Friedrich Schiller

Goethe's contributions to theater:

  • Ran the Weimar court theater for 26 years
  • Taught actors to speak in verse and act naturally
  • Made stage designs more organized and beautiful
  • Introduced ensemble acting, where the whole cast works together as a group

German Romanticism and Faust

German Romanticism began in the early 1800s, at the same time as Beethoven's heroic music. Its plays focused on emotions, nature, and imagination, were often set in far-off places or imaginary worlds, and used folklore and myths.

Goethe's Faust is one of the most famous German plays. Faust, a scholar, makes a deal with Mephistopheles, the devil: Mephistopheles will serve Faust on Earth in exchange for his soul. Faust pursues knowledge, power, and new experiences, and the play becomes a tragic story involving a young woman named Gretchen. It explores big ideas like knowledge and human nature, and mixes real-world and supernatural elements.

A movement that valued strong emotion, imagination, nature, and individual expression

Concerto
A work for a soloist and orchestra in three movements: fast, slow, fast
Soloist
The featured performer in a concerto

A showy passage the soloist plays alone, often improvised, near the end of a concerto movement

A sign (𝄐) meaning "pause," telling performers to hold a note or rest

The two expositions in a concerto's first movement, one for the orchestra and one starting with the soloist

Music for a small group, one player on each part, meant for a room in a home or palace
