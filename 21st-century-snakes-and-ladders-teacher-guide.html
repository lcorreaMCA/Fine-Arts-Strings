Learning goals

Students will be able to:

  1. Explain three roles technology plays in 21st-century art: a tool for making it, a stage for sharing it, and a source of new questions.
  2. Describe how DAWs, pitch correction, sampling and remix, virtual choirs, Vocaloid and AI, and adaptive game music have changed how music is made and heard.
  3. Connect each music idea to visual art, theater, or dance (Hockney’s iPad paintings, glitch art, the “Renegade,” streamed theater and Hamilton, Refik Anadol, teamLab, Wayne McGregor).
  4. Form and support an opinion about credit, authorship, and AI in the arts.

National Core Arts Standards anchors: Responding (Anchor 7, perceive and analyze) and Connecting (Anchor 11, relate artistic ideas to societal, cultural, and historical context).

How the game works

  • Solo play. Each student picks a game piece and plays alone against the board; there are no computer opponents.
  • Challenge squares. Students must stop on each pink square, even if they roll past it. They read the notes to the end, spend the minimum reading time (counted only while the notes are open and the tab is in front), finish the Try it, open the video and press its button, and write the 💬 answer. Then The Glitch asks three random questions from that square’s bank.
  • Wrong answers. A wrong challenge answer slides the student back 3 squares and highlights the passage with the answer; they must reread it for a short timer, then roll back for a new set of questions. A wrong Try it answer locks the buttons until they reread the highlighted part.
  • Ladders and snakes. Landing on a ladder’s foot or a snake’s head (by rolling) opens a true-or-false “Fact or glitch?” statement from a square the student has already finished. Right on a ladder: climb. Right on a snake: dodge. These are practice and are not graded, but they appear in the report.
  • Grades. Each challenge square starts at 100 and loses 10 points per wrong challenge answer (lowest 50). Grades show only after the student types the password. Rolls, ladders, and snakes never change the grade.
  • Ladders and snakes never skip an unfinished challenge square, so every student visits all seven.

Square by square

🌐 Square 5: Welcome to the digital age letter D

Student reading (summary)

In the 1980s and 1990s, people bought music on records, cassettes, and CDs, and they watched music videos on MTV, which began in 1981. Then the 21st century arrived, and the technology changed fast. In 2001 the iPod promised “1,000 songs in your pocket.” YouTube opened in 2005, the first iPhone arrived in 2007, and Spotify began streaming music in 2008. Streaming means playing music or video straight from the internet instead of owning a copy.

Art made by living artists in our own time is called contemporary art. Like the postmodernists you met in the last lesson, contemporary artists use many styles at once. What is new is how much of their work depends on technology: computers, phones, the internet, projectors, and even artificial intelligence.

Technology plays three roles in 21st-century art. It is a tool for making art: one laptop can be a whole recording studio. It is a stage for sharing art: a song can go viral online before any radio station plays it. And it raises new questions: If a computer helps make a song, who is the artist? If anyone can copy a file, who owns it?

Technology also decides what you hear next. Streaming apps use algorithms, step-by-step computer rules, to recommend songs based on what you and people like you have played and skipped. In 2018 the writer Liz Pelly found that Spotify’s recommendations repeated the gender bias of its most popular playlists, such as RapCaviar, which featured mostly men. The money is uneven too: one industry report found that in 2013 the most popular 1% of recordings earned 84% of all streaming and subscription money.

In this game, music comes first, but every square also connects music to visual art, theater, or dance. Watch for the 🔗 connection boxes.

🔗 Connection: visual art: Contemporary art is not all screens and software. The Vietnamese artist Tuan Andrew Nguyen, born in Saigon in 1976, makes films and sculptures together with Vietnamese communities around the world, including communities in Louisiana, to tell stories of colonization, war, and migration that official histories left out. The PBS series Art in the Twenty-First Century (made by Art21) follows him in its 2025 episode “Between Worlds.”

🎛️ Square 12: The bedroom studio letter I

Student reading (summary)

For most of the 20th century, making a professional record meant renting an expensive studio full of machines, with engineers to run them. Today a laptop can do the same job. A digital audio workstation, or DAW, is software for recording, editing, and mixing music. GarageBand (2004), Ableton Live, and FL Studio are popular DAWs.

A DAW lets a musician record one track at a time and stack the tracks in layers, copy and paste a part, fix a wrong note, or build a beat from short repeating patterns called loops. A MIDI controller, such as a small keyboard or a set of pads, sends notes to the computer using MIDI, a computer language for music invented in 1983. That means one small keyboard can play the sound of a piano, a string orchestra, or a drum kit.

This changed who gets to be a producer, the person who shapes how a recording sounds. Billie Eilish and her brother Finneas O’Connell recorded her first album, When We All Fall Asleep, Where Do We Go? (2019), in Finneas’s small bedroom in their family’s house in Los Angeles. In 2020, at age 18, Billie became the youngest artist ever to win the four biggest Grammy Awards in one night: Album, Record, and Song of the Year, and Best New Artist.

Even a phone can be a studio. The singer and guitarist Steve Lacy made beats on his iPhone, including the beat for Kendrick Lamar’s song “PRIDE.” (2017). Producers who work this way are often called bedroom producers.

AI has become a studio tool too. In the late 1970s John Lennon of the Beatles recorded a rough demo of a song called “Now and Then” on a cassette, with his voice and piano mixed together. In the 1990s the other Beatles could not separate his voice from the noisy piano, so the song stayed unfinished. Then a machine-learning tool built for Peter Jackson’s 2021 Beatles documentary Get Back pulled Lennon’s real voice out of the old tape. Paul McCartney and Ringo Starr finished the song in 2023, and in 2025 it won the Grammy for Best Rock Performance, the first AI-assisted recording to be nominated for a Grammy. McCartney stressed that nothing was faked: the AI cleaned up a voice that already existed.

🔗 Connection: visual art and theater: Painters went digital too. The British painter David Hockney, born in 1937, began drawing with his finger on an iPhone in 2007 and on an iPad from 2010. In the spring of 2020, at age 82, he made more than 100 iPad paintings of spring arriving at his home in Normandy, France. They were printed large and shown at the Royal Academy in London in 2021. Theater set designers moved from paper to software too. Programs such as AutoCAD, SketchUp, and Vectorworks let them build a 3D model of a set and change it in minutes instead of redrawing it by hand.

🎤 Square 19: Tuned by machine letter G

Student reading (summary)

Pitch correction is software that nudges a singer’s notes up or down until they are exactly in tune. The most famous pitch-correction program is Auto-Tune, released in 1997. It was invented by Andy Hildebrand, an engineer who had used math to study sound waves deep underground while searching for oil.

Used gently, Auto-Tune is almost impossible to hear, and many recordings use it to fix small mistakes. But on Cher’s song “Believe” (1998), the producers turned the setting to its most extreme. Her notes jumped instantly from pitch to pitch, and her voice sounded like a robot. Listeners loved the strange sound, and it became an effect of its own.

In the 2000s, the singer T-Pain made the robotic Auto-Tune sound his trademark, and many rap and pop artists copied it. Some musicians pushed back: in 2009, Jay-Z released a song called “D.O.A. (Death of Auto-Tune).” The argument continues today. Is pitch correction cheating, or is it just another instrument, like the electric guitar once was?

Machines changed the voice even earlier. The vocoder, invented at Bell Labs in the 1930s to send voices over telephone lines, turns a voice into a robotic, synthesizer-like sound. Musicians from the German band Kraftwerk to the French duo Daft Punk later used vocoders in their songs.

🔗 Connection: visual art: Photos get “tuned” too. Apps can smooth skin, change colors, or erase objects from a picture, and many people now ask how much editing is too much. Some artists do the opposite. In glitch art, they break digital files on purpose and turn the errors, such as broken pixels, streaks, and scrambled colors, into art.

🔁 Square 26: Gone viral: remix and the internet letter I

Student reading (summary)

You met sampling in the hip-hop of the 1970s: borrowing a short recorded piece of an older song to build a new one. A remix is a new version of a song, made by changing its beat, its parts, or its singers. A mashup combines two or more songs into one track. With a DAW and the internet, anyone can sample, remix, and share music with the world.

In 2018, Montero Lamar Hill, a 19-year-old from the Atlanta area who calls himself Lil Nas X, bought a beat online for about $30. A teenage producer in the Netherlands called YoungKio had made it from a sample of banjo music by the rock band Nine Inch Nails. Lil Nas X rapped and sang over it about riding a horse, and he called his country-rap song “Old Town Road.”

The song spread through TikTok, where people made short videos of themselves turning into cowboys. Billboard removed it from its country chart, saying it was not country enough, and that started a big argument about who decides what a genre is. Then Lil Nas X released a remix with the country singer Billy Ray Cyrus. In 2019, “Old Town Road” spent 19 weeks at number one on the Billboard Hot 100, a record at the time.

Because the beat uses Nine Inch Nails’ music, the band’s Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross are listed as songwriters of “Old Town Road.” Borrowing music the right way means asking permission and giving credit.

The internet also carries music across borders and languages. In 2018, eight of the ten most-watched music videos on YouTube worldwide were in Spanish, and the top two were reggaeton songs by Latin artists from the United States, Puerto Rico, and Colombia. That year Beyoncé joined the Colombian singer J Balvin on stage at the Coachella festival to perform their remix of his song “Mi Gente.”

🔗 Connection: dance: Dances go viral too, and credit matters there as well. In 2019, Jalaiah Harmon, a 14-year-old dancer from the Atlanta area, created the “Renegade” dance. Millions of people copied it on TikTok, usually without knowing who made it, until a 2020 New York Times article told her story. Soon after, she was invited to perform it at the NBA All-Star Game.

🎶 Square 33: Together, apart letter T

Student reading (summary)

In 2009, the American composer Eric Whitacre saw a video of a young woman singing one of his choral pieces alone in front of her webcam. It gave him an idea: what if hundreds of singers did the same thing, each recording a part at home, and all their videos were joined into one choir?

In 2010 he released his first Virtual Choir: 185 singers from 12 countries singing his piece Lux Aurumque (Latin for “light and gold”). Each singer put on headphones and watched a video of Whitacre conducting, then recorded a part alone and uploaded the video. Finally, an editor lined up every voice and every video so they sounded and looked like one choir.

The choirs kept growing. In 2020, while the COVID-19 pandemic kept people at home, Virtual Choir 6 sang Whitacre’s “Sing Gently” with 17,572 singers from 129 countries.

Why not just sing together on a video call? Because of latency, the tiny delay as sound travels across the internet. Even a delay of a fraction of a second makes singers fall out of time with each other. That is why virtual choirs record separately and are lined up later.

🔗 Connection: theater: The pandemic closed theaters around the world in March 2020. Broadway stayed dark for 18 months, until September 2021. Many theaters streamed recorded shows instead; London’s National Theatre put a free play on YouTube each week. In July 2020, a filmed version of Lin-Manuel Miranda’s musical Hamilton (2015), which tells the story of the founding father Alexander Hamilton with hip-hop, R&B, and Broadway songs, came out on Disney+, so millions of people saw it at home. But going online can also leave people out. A 2025 study of theater audiences in Nigeria found that 68% of people aged 18 to 35 preferred livestreamed or online plays, yet only 22% of the people surveyed in rural areas had reliable internet. This gap is called the digital divide.

🤖 Square 40: Artificial voices letter A

Student reading (summary)

AI music is older than you might think. Composers have used rules, dice, and machines to make music for centuries, and the first computer music was played in 1951. Click through the history below.

Some 21st-century singers are not people at all. Vocaloid is singing software made by the Yamaha company: you type in a melody and words, and it sings them in a voice built from recordings of a real singer. In 2007, the Japanese company Crypton Future Media released a Vocaloid voice called Hatsune Miku, made from the voice of the actress Saki Fujita. It came with a cartoon character: a 16-year-old girl with long turquoise pigtails.

Fans, not the company, made Miku famous. Thousands of amateur composers wrote songs for her and posted them online. Today she “performs” concerts with a live band while her animated image is projected onto a clear screen on stage, so she seems to float like a hologram.

Since 2022, AI (artificial intelligence) tools that create pictures, writing, and music from a short typed request have become available to the public. AI systems learn by studying huge amounts of existing art, music, and writing, called training data. Many artists have objected that their work was used to train these systems without their permission or any payment.

In April 2023, an anonymous TikTok user called Ghostwriter977 released “Heart on My Sleeve,” a song with AI vocals made to sound like the rapper Drake and the singer the Weeknd, who had nothing to do with it. It drew millions of views and plays within days. Then it was pulled from Spotify, Apple Music, and YouTube after a copyright claim by Universal Music Group, the record company of both stars. An AI copy of a real person’s voice is called a voice clone.

Musicians are deciding how to use these tools. The American composer Holly Herndon had already used an AI she called Spawn on her 2019 album PROTO. She trained an AI on her own voice and in 2021 released Holly+, a “digital twin” of her voice that other people can use to sing. The Recording Academy, which gives the Grammy Awards, decided in 2023 that only human creators can be nominated. A song made with some help from AI can be considered only if people did a meaningful part of the work.

Some composers are trying to use AI more fairly. For his 2025 orchestra piece Techno-utopia, the British composer Robert Laidlow trained AI only on about 2,500 hours of BBC Radio 3 broadcasts by one orchestra, the BBC Philharmonic, used with permission, instead of on music gathered from the whole internet. Laidlow points out a problem many people miss: most recordings are owned by record companies, not by the artists, so a company can allow AI to train on a song even if the singer never agreed.

Across all the arts, AI shows up in three ways. It can be a repair tool that cleans up or fixes real work, like the Beatles’ “Now and Then.” It can be a creative partner that an artist chooses to work with, like Holly+ or Robert Laidlow’s orchestra AI. Or it can be a fake copy that imitates artists without asking, like “Heart on My Sleeve.” In painting, film, and music alike, people keep asking the same three questions: Did the artists agree? Are they paid? Who gets the credit?

🔗 Connection: visual art: In 2022, the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) in New York showed Unsupervised by the Turkish-American artist Refik Anadol. He trained an AI on images of more than 200 years of art in MoMA’s collection, and the AI created a giant, ever-changing digital “painting” that flowed across a wall of the museum’s lobby.

🎮 Square 46: Step inside the art letter L

Student reading (summary)

Immersive art surrounds you, so you step inside it instead of looking at it in a frame. In Tokyo, the art group teamLab, founded in 2001, opened teamLab Borderless in 2018: dark rooms filled with projected digital flowers, waterfalls, and lights that drift from room to room and change when visitors touch them or walk past. More than two million people visited in its first year, a world record for a museum devoted to a single art group.

Music in video games has to be immersive too. In a movie the music never changes, but in a game nobody knows what the player will do next. So composers write adaptive music, which changes as you play. It may add drums when an enemy appears, grow quiet while you explore, or switch to a victory theme when you win. Composers often write it in layers, like the tracks in a DAW, and the game turns the layers on and off.

Game music now fills concert halls, and orchestras around the world give whole concerts of music from games such as The Legend of Zelda and Final Fantasy. In 2013, Austin Wintory’s score for the game Journey (2012) became the first video game soundtrack ever nominated for a Grammy. In 2023, the Grammys added an award just for video game music. Its first winner was the composer Stephanie Economou, for Assassin’s Creed Valhalla: Dawn of Ragnarök.

In the 21st century, music often arrives inside another digital art form: an app, a video game, or a concert where the performers are not really there. Click through three examples.

🔗 Connection: dance: The British choreographer Wayne McGregor worked with Google Arts & Culture to train an AI on videos from his dance company’s 25-year archive. The tool, called Living Archive (2019), suggests new movements in McGregor’s style, and his dancers try them out and build them into new choreography.

🔗 Connection: theater: Theaters use computers too. Automated stage machinery can move scenery smoothly and fly large objects, even a full-size car over the audience in a stage production of Chitty Chitty Bang Bang. A person still runs the control desk, though, because live actors never do exactly the same thing every night. In interactive theater, sensors let performers trigger sounds and projected images with their own movements.
