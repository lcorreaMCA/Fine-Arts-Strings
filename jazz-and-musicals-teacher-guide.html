Teacher Guide: Jazz & Broadway Swim Meet

Fine Arts Survey, 8th grade. One 30-minute lesson. Music first, with dance and visual-art connections.

At a glance

Student filejazz-and-musicals.html
FormatSwim meet game based on the Genially swimming race template. Four lanes, open in any order, each with an animated swimmer whose arms and legs move as the student works. In each lane the student must read the cards first, then finish every activity, then win a timed race. Each finished lane lights one letter of the finish code.
Reading firstThe activities stay locked until the student has spent a minimum reading time on the lane’s cards (about half of normal reading time, 45 seconds to 2½ minutes depending on the lane) and scrolled to the end of the notes. The timer only runs while the cards are on screen and the tab is in front.
Wrong answers in activitiesEvery activity works like the race: a wrong answer highlights the paragraph(s) that hold the answer and scrolls the student there. The activity’s buttons (and every other activity’s buttons in that lane) stay locked until the student has kept the highlighted text on screen for 10–40 seconds, depending on its length. Their answers stay in place, so they only fix the wrong ones. This includes matching, ordering, the Seurat question, and the mystery rhythms in the syncopation lab (which send students to the ragtime paragraph). The PDF report lists each reread.
The race4 questions in a row, 20 seconds each. A wrong answer or a timeout sends the swimmer back to the wall and highlights the paragraph that holds the answer. The student must keep that paragraph on screen for 10–30 seconds (based on its length) before racing again with new questions, the same reread rule as the earlier lessons.
Final lapAfter the fourth lane, a “Final lap” window opens with four short-answer questions. Each answer needs at least 10 words before the student can submit, and each box shows a sentence starter. Answers save automatically, so a student can close the window and reopen it from the “Final lap” button on the scoreboard. Submitting touches the wall and downloads the PDF, which includes all four answers for you to grade.
Finish codeSCAT (lanes light S, C, A, T in any order)
GradesHidden until the end. Each lane starts at 100, minus 10 for each race miss and 2 for each activity item missed on the first try (lowest lane grade 50). The overall grade is the average of the four lanes.

Learning goals

  • Explain how the mix of cultures in New Orleans, including Congo Square, brass bands, ragtime, and blues, led to jazz.
  • Identify syncopation by ear and define improvisation, collective improvisation, and scat singing.
  • Put the main jazz styles in order and connect key musicians to them.
  • Describe how the Broadway musical grew from ballad opera, operetta, vaudeville, early musical comedy (Harrigan and Hart), and revue (the Ziegfeld Follies) into the integrated book musical.
  • Explain the jobs songs do in a musical and give examples from Show Boat, Oklahoma!, and West Side Story.
  • Connect musicals to dance (Agnes de Mille, Jerome Robbins), visual art (Seurat and Sunday in the Park with George), literature (Shakespeare, Shaw, Hugo), film, and jazz.

Short answers (Final lap)

Simple recall-and-explain questions written for 8th graders. Each box shows a sentence starter, and students need at least 10 words (one or two sentences). They are not auto-graded; they appear in each student’s PDF. A simple scale: 2 = correct with a detail from the lesson, 1 = partly correct or very general, 0 = off topic or missing (8 points total).

  1. Name one kind of music from New Orleans that helped create jazz. What is one thing you learned about it?
    Any one of: ragtime (syncopated right hand, Scott Joplin), the blues (12 bars, blue notes, Bessie Smith), brass bands (parades, funerals, the second line), Congo Square drumming and dancing, spirituals or call and response.
  2. In your own words, how is a book musical different from a variety show like vaudeville?
    Book musical: has a story, and the songs and dances help tell it. Vaudeville: separate acts (songs, comedy, jugglers, animals) with no story.
  3. Name another musical from the lesson and tell what story, play, or painting it is based on.
    The Lion King → Hamlet; Kiss Me, Kate → The Taming of the Shrew; My Fair Lady → Pygmalion; Les Misérables → Victor Hugo’s novel; Oklahoma! → Green Grow the Lilacs; Sunday in the Park with George → Seurat’s painting; Show Boat → (novel, if they know it); Disney musicals → the Disney films.
  4. Which poster do you like better, the movie poster or the Broadway poster? Explain why.
    Any opinion earns credit if it names the show and gives a reason based on what the poster shows (colors, characters, a simple symbol, the mood).

Answer key

Lane 1: Roots in New Orleans (letter S)

  • Syncopation lab: Mystery 1 On the beat; Mystery 2 Syncopated; Mystery 3 Syncopated (3 + 3 + 2 pattern). Part 3 is complete when the student places at least two snare accents on “&” spots, plays the rhythm, and checks it. Sounds (bass drum, hi-hat, snare) are made in the browser, so no audio files are needed. The yellow boxes show the accents after each answer, so a muted device still works.
  • match each ingredient: Congo Square → West African drumming and circle dances; Second line → People dancing behind a brass band; Ragtime → Syncopated piano music by Scott Joplin; Blues → A 12-measure pattern with bent “blue notes”
  • Race bank (11 questions; each race draws 4, favoring ones the student hasn’t seen):
    1. Where did enslaved Africans and free people of color gather on Sundays to drum and dance? Congo Square (highlights: “Congo Square”)
    2. What did the city rule of 1817 do? Allowed the Sunday gatherings in only one place, Congo Square (highlights: “Congo Square”)
    3. Why did visitors come from far away to see the Congo Square dances? African drumming had been banned in most other Southern states (highlights: “Congo Square”)
    4. What did the 1894 law do? It classified Creoles of color as Black, pushing two musical worlds together (highlights: “Two musical worlds come together”)
    5. What is the main feature of ragtime? A syncopated right-hand melody over a steady left hand (highlights: “The ingredients”)
    6. Who was called the “King of Ragtime”? Scott Joplin (highlights: “The ingredients”)
    7. In New Orleans, the “second line” is… the crowd dancing behind a brass band (highlights: “The ingredients”)
    8. How many measures (bars) are in the most common blues pattern? 12 (highlights: “The ingredients”)
    9. Who was the “Empress of the Blues”? Bessie Smith (highlights: “The ingredients”)
    10. Call and response came from… West African music and African American church services (highlights: “The ingredients”)
    11. What did Congress create in 1994? The New Orleans Jazz National Historical Park (highlights: “A city where cultures met”)

Lane 2: Jazz Takes Off (letter C)

  • put the styles in order: New Orleans style (Dixieland) → Swing → Bebop → Cool jazz → Free jazz → Jazz rock (fusion)
  • who am I?: I learned cornet at the Colored Waifs Home and made scat singing famous. → Louis Armstrong; I was the “Empress of the Blues” and the most famous blues singer of the 1920s. → Bessie Smith; I led one of the most famous big bands of the swing era. → Duke Ellington; I played alto saxophone and led the fast, complex style called bebop. → Charlie Parker
  • Race bank (11 questions; each race draws 4, favoring ones the student hasn’t seen):
    1. What do we call it when several musicians make up different melodies at the same time? Collective improvisation (highlights: “The first jazz bands”)
    2. Who was the first famous jazz bandleader? Charles “Buddy” Bolden (highlights: “The first jazz bands”)
    3. When was the first jazz record made? 1917 (highlights: “The first jazz bands”)
    4. Singing a melody with made-up syllables instead of words is called… scat singing (highlights: “Louis Armstrong, jazz’s first superstar”)
    5. Where did Louis Armstrong learn to play the cornet? The band at the Colored Waifs Home (highlights: “Louis Armstrong, jazz’s first superstar”)
    6. Who called Louis Armstrong to Chicago in 1922? Joe “King” Oliver (highlights: “Louis Armstrong, jazz’s first superstar”)
    7. Who was Lil Hardin Armstrong? A pianist and composer who wrote tunes for the band (highlights: “Louis Armstrong, jazz’s first superstar”)
    8. Which style did big bands play from 1935 to 1945? Swing (highlights: “A timeline of jazz styles”)
    9. Who led the fast, complex style called bebop? Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie (highlights: “A timeline of jazz styles”)
    10. Jazz rock (fusion) mixed jazz improvisation with… rock rhythms and electric instruments (highlights: “A timeline of jazz styles”)
    11. Why are the 1920s known as “the Jazz Age”? Records and radio carried jazz everywhere (highlights: “Jazz on the move”)

Lane 3: Birth of the Broadway Musical (letter A)

  • build the timeline: The Beggar’s Opera (ballad opera) → The Black Crook → Harrigan and Hart’s musical comedies → The first Ziegfeld Follies → Jerome Kern’s Princess Theatre musicals → Show Boat
  • old show or book musical?: Songs could be moved to a different show and no one would notice. → Older variety show or operetta; Songs reveal what a character feels and move the story forward. → Book musical; Jugglers, comedians, and animal acts with no plot at all. → Older variety show or operetta; A song reveals a character’s secret and changes her life, as in Show Boat. → Book musical; Elaborate costumes and a chorus line between loosely connected comedy sketches. → Older variety show or operetta; Recognizable characters in modern-day American situations. → Book musical
  • Race bank (15 questions; each race draws 4, favoring ones the student hasn’t seen):
    1. What is the “book” of a book musical? The script, which gives the show a strong plot (highlights: “What is a Broadway book musical?”)
    2. In a musical, “integration” means… weaving story, songs, dance, and character together (highlights: “What is a Broadway book musical?”)
    3. How is a musical usually different from an opera? Musicals mix spoken scenes with songs; operas are mostly sung (highlights: “What is a Broadway book musical?”)
    4. What did ballad operas like The Beggar’s Opera do? Put new, funny words to familiar tunes to poke fun at politics (highlights: “The ancestors of the musical”)
    5. Which description fits operetta? Light, romantic opera, often set in faraway, exotic places (highlights: “The ancestors of the musical”)
    6. How were Gilbert and Sullivan different from most operetta writers? Their songs fit the story, though their plots were fantastical (highlights: “The ancestors of the musical”)
    7. How did The Black Crook (1866) come about? A stranded ballet company was added to a melodrama about magic (highlights: “First steps on Broadway”)
    8. Who created the first American musical comedies on Broadway in the 1870s? Harrigan and Hart (highlights: “First steps on Broadway”)
    9. Who produced a new Follies revue every year from 1907 to 1931? Florenz Ziegfeld (highlights: “First steps on Broadway”)
    10. Who is often called the “Father of the Book Musical”? Jerome Kern (highlights: “The book musical is born”)
    11. Why was Show Boat (1927) groundbreaking? It told a serious story about racism, with songs that fit the characters (highlights: “The book musical is born”)
    12. In Show Boat, what does Julie’s song “Can’t Help Lovin’ Dat Man” do? It gives the first clue to a secret that changes her life (highlights: “The book musical is born”)
    13. Which song from Porgy and Bess became one of the most recorded jazz standards? “Summertime” (highlights: “Where jazz meets Broadway”)
    14. Who recorded songs from Porgy and Bess together in 1957? Ella Fitzgerald and Louis Armstrong (highlights: “Where jazz meets Broadway”)
    15. In 1964, whose recording of “Hello, Dolly!” knocked the Beatles off number one? Louis Armstrong (highlights: “Where jazz meets Broadway”)

Lane 4: The Golden Age and Beyond (letter T)

  • name the song’s job: Early in the show, a poor flower seller sings about wishing for a warm room and enough to eat. → “I Want” song; After a tense scene, a dancing teapot and singing dishes put on a silly dinner show. → Comic number; Two gangs, a couple in love, and a girl planning her evening all sing at once, each with a different feeling. → Ensemble number; A father-to-be starts talking about his future child, and his feelings grow so big that the talking turns into a song. → Breaking into song
  • where did the story come from?: West Side Story → Shakespeare’s Romeo and Juliet; The Lion King → Shakespeare’s Hamlet; Kiss Me, Kate → Shakespeare’s The Taming of the Shrew; My Fair Lady → Shaw’s play Pygmalion; Les Misérables → Victor Hugo’s novel
  • look closely at Seurat: With thousands of tiny dots of color placed side by side
  • Race bank (18 questions; each race draws 4, favoring ones the student hasn’t seen):
    1. When was Broadway’s Golden Age? The 1940s to the early 1960s (highlights: “The Golden Age of Broadway”)
    2. Who wrote Oklahoma! (1943)? Rodgers and Hammerstein (highlights: “The Golden Age of Broadway”)
    3. Why do the songs in Oklahoma! sound like the characters talking? Hammerstein wrote the lyrics first (highlights: “The Golden Age of Broadway”)
    4. What did Agnes de Mille’s dream ballet in Oklahoma! do? Showed the heroine’s feelings and told part of the story through dance (highlights: “The Golden Age of Broadway”)
    5. What does an “I Want” song do? Tells us early what the main character wants most (highlights: “Every song has a job”)
    6. Why do characters in a musical break into song? Their feelings get too big for ordinary talking (highlights: “Every song has a job”)
    7. West Side Story retells which story? Romeo and Juliet (highlights: “West Side Story (1957)”)
    8. How did Jerome Robbins first imagine the lovers in West Side Story? As a Jewish girl and an Italian Catholic boy on New York’s East Side (highlights: “West Side Story (1957)”)
    9. Which West Side Story song borrows the sound of bebop jazz? “Cool” (highlights: “West Side Story (1957)”)
    10. Which bandleader won a Grammy for a big-band jazz album of West Side Story? Stan Kenton (highlights: “West Side Story (1957)”)
    11. Which musical borrows the plot of Shakespeare’s Hamlet? The Lion King (highlights: “From page to stage”)
    12. My Fair Lady came from Shaw’s play Pygmalion, named after a myth about… a sculptor whose statue comes to life (highlights: “From page to stage”)
    13. Which Sound of Music song did John Coltrane turn into a famous jazz piece? “My Favorite Things” (highlights: “Screen to stage, and back again”)
    14. What was the first Disney film turned into a Broadway musical (1994)? Beauty and the Beast (highlights: “Screen to stage, and back again”)
    15. What made Julie Taymor’s Lion King (1997) famous? Her giant masks and puppets; she was the first woman to win the Tony for directing a musical (highlights: “Screen to stage, and back again”)
    16. Which Disney film flopped in theaters but became a Broadway hit in 2012? Newsies (highlights: “Screen to stage, and back again”)
    17. What was the first rock musical? Hair (1967) (highlights: “After 1960”)
    18. Sunday in the Park with George imagines the life of which painter? Georges Seurat (highlights: “After 1960”)
